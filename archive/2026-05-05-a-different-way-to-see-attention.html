Just to clarify up front: this article is not meant for total beginners who don’t already know the basics of attention and Transformers.

This is for people who already know the standard story.

You know the standard story. Attention gets explained as an architecture that can be broken down into queries, keys, and values, and these allow tokens (words) to attend to each other to understand context. That’s how it’s taught in courses, in YouTube videos, even in proper university lectures. The explanation isn’t wrong — the QKV decomposition is mathematically what’s happening — but I recently learned something that gave me a different angle on it, and I don’t see this angle discussed often.

I’m writing this because I found this perspective useful, especially as a student new to LLM research who’s curious (and maybe bold) enough to think the Transformer architecture isn’t the final answer and could be improved. If you want to improve something, it helps to understand why it works in the first place — not just how the math goes, but what problem it was responding to.

So I went back to where it started. “Attention Is All You Need,” 2017. I tried to figure out what specifically the authors were responding to.

The clue is in the blog post, not the paper

The original paper doesn’t really explain why attention is the right move. It explains what attention is and shows it works but the motivation is somewhat implicit.

The Google Research blog post that accompanied the paper is much clearer about the underlying problem. It cites a 2001 paper called Gradient Flow in Recurrent Nets: the Difficulty of Learning Long-Term Dependencies, by Sepp Hochreiter, Yoshua Bengio, Paolo Frasconi, and Jürgen Schmidhuber. The blog post talks about the difficulty RNN architectures had with learning long-term dependencies between words.

Reading that paper helped me see attention differently. Instead of looking like an arbitrary architectural choice, it started to look like a fairly direct response to a specific, well-characterized problem. That reframe is what I want to share.

Let me explain what the Hochreiter paper actually says.

What the Hochreiter paper actually proves

The setup is simple. An RNN processes a sequence one step at a time, maintaining a hidden state that gets updated at each step. To learn a long-range dependency — say, that a verb at position 50 should agree with a noun at position 2 — the network needs gradient descent to push an error signal backward through time, from the loss at position 50 all the way back to the weights that decided what to remember about position 2.

The Hochreiter analysis asks a precise question: as the error signal flows backward through time, what mathematically happens to it?

To make this concrete, the paper writes the error signal at unit j, time τ as δⱼ(τ) = ∂E / ∂netⱼ(τ) — basically, “how much does a small change in this unit’s input change the loss?” Then it asks how an error signal at output unit k at time t scales when it propagates back to some earlier unit v at time s. By unrolling the chain rule through time, you get the scaling factor:

\[\frac{\partial \delta_v(s)}{\partial \delta_k(t)} = \sum_{l_{t-1}=1}^{n} \cdots \sum_{l_{s+1}=1}^{n} \left( w_{l_t l_{t-1}} \prod_{\tau=t-1}^{s+1} f’_{l_\tau}(\text{net}_{l\tau}(\tau)) , w_{l_\tau l_{\tau-1}} \right) f’_{l_s}(\text{net}_{l_s}(s))\]

The sums run over all possible “paths” of intermediate units the error could travel through. For each path, you multiply together t − s factors of the form f′(net) · w — one factor per time step the error has to traverse.

This product structure is what drives the rest of the analysis. Products of many small numbers vanish exponentially. Products of many large numbers explode exponentially. There’s only a narrow regime where the product stays bounded.

The paper makes it concrete with sigmoid activations: f′ peaks at 0.25, so unless the weight is greater than 4.0, each factor is below 1 and the product collapses fast. Tightening this with matrix norms, the paper shows that if ‖W‖ₐ < 4.0 (roughly: weights aren’t too large), the scaling factor satisfies

\[\left| \frac{\partial \delta_v(s)}{\partial \delta_k(t)} \right| \leq n \cdot \lambda^{t-s}, \quad \text{where } \lambda < 1\]

Exponential decay in the time lag t − s. By the time you’re trying to send error back fifty time steps, the signal has been multiplied by something like λ⁵⁰ — astronomically small. That’s the vanishing gradient.

That’s the Section 2 result of the paper. Section 3 makes it worse.

The deeper result generalizes the analysis to any system that stores information by maintaining a state inside an attractor’s basin. Robust storage requires the dynamics to be contractive — small perturbations must shrink, otherwise noise corrupts the memory. Mathematically, this means the Jacobian norm of the state-update map must be less than 1.

But the same Jacobian appears in the gradient computation. When forward dynamics are contractive (good for memory), the backward gradient decays exponentially (bad for learning).

This is a dilemma, not just a difficulty. The very property you need for robust storage is the property that prevents you from learning what to store. You can have one or the other, but not both — at least, not in a system with a fixed transition map.

That’s the obstacle the paper identifies. A lot of architecture work between 2001 and 2017 can be read as different attempts to work around it.

How attention works around it

Now read attention with this in mind.

In an RNN, when information needs to get from position s to position t, it physically passes through the hidden state at every intermediate step. Position s‘s information goes into position s+1‘s state, which goes into position s+2‘s state, and so on. By the time you reach t, the information has been re-encoded t − s times. Each re-encoding multiplies a Jacobian, and the product is exactly what the Hochreiter analysis is about.

But you can imagine a different design. What if, instead of compressing the past into a single evolving state, you just kept everything? What if at every position, you kept the full intermediate representation, separately, and let later positions reach back and look at any earlier representation directly when they needed it?

That’s what attention does.

In a Transformer, every position has its own residual stream. Position 50’s information doesn’t travel through positions 3, 4, 5, ..., 49 to reach position 50’s output. Position 50 just looks at position 2 directly, in a single attention operation. The gradient path is one hop, regardless of distance.

There’s no chain of t − s Jacobians along the sequence axis because there’s no chain at all along that axis. The architecture doesn’t compose information across positions step by step. Instead, it pays a different cost: storing every intermediate representation for every token, at every layer, in every attention head, and computing all-pairs interactions between them.

This is the trade. RNNs compress everything into one running state, which is cheap in memory and compute but pays the Hochreiter cost. Transformers keep representations separately at each position, which is expensive in memory and compute (quadratic in sequence length) but avoids the sequence-axis version of that cost.

When you put it this way, attention isn’t just about “tokens attending to each other to understand context” — that’s a description of what the math does, not why the math is structured that way. One way to read attention is as a choice to not compress information across time. The QKV decomposition is the specific mechanism for the lookup; the residual stream is the specific data structure that holds the per-position information; the multi-head structure is the specific way of allowing multiple parallel lookups. But the underlying move, the thing that makes the architecture handle long ranges so differently from RNNs, is that it doesn’t compose state across positions in the first place.

That’s the reframe I want to share. Attention can be read as the architectural answer to “what if we just didn’t compose state across positions?” Once you see it this way, a few things click into place.

Two implications that changed how I read papers

This perspective gave me two things I didn’t have before.

One: a constraint to read the field through

Before learning about the Hochreiter paper, I had a vague sense that “signals deteriorate in deep models” but no precise framework. I couldn’t tell when deterioration was a real obstacle versus an artifact of bad training, when an architectural change was solving a deep problem versus just rearranging the surface. The literature felt like a parade of unrelated tricks, each justified by empirical results but conceptually disconnected.

The Hochreiter paper gives me a theoretical reference point that lets me read architecture papers as responses to a precise question. The question is: how do you do long, coordinated, learnable computation, given that composing many transformations under shared parameters causes gradient pathologies?

Once I have this lens, I can read many modern ML innovations as instances of one of about five strategies for handling that obstacle:

Direct addressing. Don’t compose at all when you don’t have to. Attention itself is the canonical example — every position can reach any other in one hop, so there’s no chain to multiply. Retrieval-augmented generation extends this idea to external corpora. Mixture-of-Experts applies it to parameter selection. Pointer networks apply it to outputs. The strategy is: when composition is expensive, replace it with lookup.

Pinned Jacobians. When you must compose, design each step to be close to the identity. Residual connections do this: hₗ₊₁ = hₗ + f(hₗ) has Jacobian I + f′, which stays close to I regardless of how many layers you stack. ResNet, the Transformer’s residual stream, Highway Networks, DenseNet — all the same conceptual move, applied in different places. State-space models like Mamba carefully engineer their state-transition matrix to have eigenvalues just inside the unit circle, which is the same idea: stay near the boundary where composition is nearly free.

Learnable gated recurrence. When you must compose, learn when each composition step is free vs. costly. LSTM does this with multiplicative gates that decide per-timestep how much of the cell state to preserve and how much to overwrite. GRU is a simpler variant. xLSTM is a recent revival. Modern selective state-space models are gated recurrence reborn for the post-Transformer era. The strategy: don’t fix the trade-off; let the network learn when to be in latching mode and when to be in transmission mode.

Externalized state. Replace internal composition with external generation. Instead of running a long reasoning chain inside hidden states, write it down as tokens. Each step gets a fresh forward pass through the full network and can attend to all previous steps directly. Chain-of-thought prompting, scratchpad training, agentic loops, and reasoning models like o1 are all instances. The strategy: when internal composition is too expensive, externalize it into the visible token sequence where attention can address it directly.

Hierarchical composition. Break a long chain into multiple shorter chains operating at different scales. WaveNet’s dilated convolutions, U-Nets, hierarchical RNNs, multi-scale Transformers like Longformer and BigBird, hierarchical Mixture-of-Experts. The strategy: when a single chain would be too long, decompose it.

This isn’t a unified theory of everything — there are real architectural innovations that don’t fit cleanly into these categories. But it covers enough to be a useful lens, and it gives me a way to ask “what’s actually new here?” about a paper rather than just “what’s claimed?”

Two: a different way to see KV cache

This implication is more personal but also more practical for me.

Before, KV cache sounded like some “optimization trick” — something engineers cared about for inference speed but conceptually uninteresting. I didn’t pay much attention to it.

Reading the Hochreiter paper made me reconsider this. The keys and values stored at each position, at each layer, at each attention head, can be thought of as the model’s state at that position. They’re the representations the model has built up by that layer about that token, and they’re what every subsequent computation reads from when this position is attended to.

Once I see KV cache as state representations rather than engineering scaffolding, a few things become interesting. It’s also worth noting that QKV states are all derived from the residual stream — they’re projections of an even more “compressed” representation. So if I want to understand what the model is really storing at each position, the residual stream itself is probably the more fundamental object, and the KV cache is one view into it.

The Hochreiter problem probably still exists in Transformers — just along another axis

The state at position t at layer ℓ is built from the state at position t at layer ℓ−1 through attention and MLP operations. So even though there’s no recurrence across positions, there’s a kind of “vertical” recurrence within each position, evolving through depth rather than time. The residual stream at each position is itself a dynamical system — its “time” axis is just layer index instead of sequence position.

The Hochreiter-style analysis can be applied to this vertical evolution directly. What saves it is residual connections: they pin the layer-to-layer Jacobian close to identity, so the depth-wise product doesn’t collapse or explode. The Transformer didn’t avoid the Hochreiter dilemma entirely — it traded sequence-axis composition (which it doesn’t do) for depth-axis composition (which it does, but managed via residuals). The dilemma is still there, just in a form the architecture is built to handle.

Closing thought

The standard explanation of attention — queries, keys, values, tokens attending to each other — describes what the math does. The Hochreiter framing adds something different: a sense of what problem the math was responding to.

Read this way, attention is one specific trade — give up the compression of state across positions, accept the quadratic cost — that turned out to work well enough to launch (almost) a decade of progress. It’s not the only possible trade, and it doesn’t solve every long-range problem. The Hochreiter dilemma still appears along the depth axis (mostly handled by residual connections), and tasks requiring genuine iterative composition still hit walls (partially handled by chain-of-thought).

For me, that calibration is the most useful thing I’ve gotten from reading the paper. The math is twenty-five years old, but the frame it provides for reading the field still feels current. Whether or not this perspective is the right way to see attention, it’s been a useful one for me, and I wanted to write it down.
